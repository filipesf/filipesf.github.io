Shipping seller flows on a payments platform that does not tolerate type drift

Seven months in a B2B fintech monorepo, building the wizards, wallets, and reconciliation code that have to agree with the database at every step.

At a glance

Problem
Seller onboarding, wallet visibility, and the fee handshake between platform and payment gateway had drifted. Stale defaults were overwriting real values, and zero-fee transactions were silently being charged.
Role
Senior Frontend Engineer across payments and products. Fastify endpoints, Kysely queries, TypeBox schemas, and the React surfaces that consume them.
Stack

Impact
A KYC wizard that survives reloads, a wallet that returns honest numbers, and a fee reconciliation that stopped booking zero-fee transactions at the default rate.

Context

The platform is an all-in-one digital marketing stack: sales pages, checkout, CRM, affiliates, messaging across WhatsApp, Telegram, and email, payments, funnels, workflow automation. It runs as a TypeScript monorepo. The defining technical choice is the type-safety pipeline. Data shape is declared in Prisma, queried with Kysely, validated with TypeBox, exposed via Fastify with OpenAPI, and consumed in React through Orval-generated SDK hooks. Break the chain anywhere and the contract degrades silently. Keep it intact and the IDE catches drift before it ships.

I joined as Senior Frontend Engineer with primary residency in the payments and products domains. The codebase doesn’t really tolerate a frontend-only lane though. Every interesting product slice ends up touching a Fastify endpoint, a Kysely query, or a TypeBox schema. The shape of the work was full-stack inside specific domains, not half-stack across everything.

What was waiting on me

A backlog clustered around payments-domain pain that had been deferred while the platform shipped marketing and content features. The categories that dominated the first six months: a seller KYC onboarding flow that couldn’t survive a reload, wallet and reporting surfaces sellers couldn’t get a straight answer out of, a fee reconciliation between the platform and its primary payment gateway that was using stale default constants instead of the real values returned per transaction, and a long tail of pending-status banners and recovery flows scattered ad hoc across product, wallet, and KYC.

None of these were greenfield. Every one of them sat on top of existing code that had reached the edge of its design, and the work was finding the seam where the next iteration could attach without breaking what already shipped.

Approach

Each domain became its own spec problem. Linear tickets defined the contract before any code moved. The PR body explained the choice. Branches followed an author-prefixed scope so the history was greppable later. Conventional Commits with project-specific scopes (feat(payments), fix(payments), refactor(payments)) carried intent through merge and made the changelog readable at a glance.

The pipeline was the discipline. Backend endpoints with explicit operationIds so the generated SDK hook had a stable name in the frontend. Vitest coverage at the service layer for anything fee-related, anything that affected a balance, anything that mutated a transaction. Critical seller and checkout flows lived under Playwright by the time they shipped.

The choice not to be loud was deliberate. The platform was already mid-flight and nobody needed a new opinion about how to organise components. The work that mattered was unblocking sellers, fixing the silent failures, and leaving the type chain intact.

The KYC wizard

A five-step flow guiding new sellers through account type, identity data, bank and PIX details, document upload, and review. The shaping decision was backend-first with a draft fallback. Every step persists to the server immediately. A per-user localStorage draft (7-day TTL) only holds the form when the backend isn’t ready to accept it. Per-step gateway validation surfaces errors at the boundary instead of letting them pile up to the final submit. Field locks engage after submission so a seller in review can’t accidentally invalidate their own record.

Reuse was the secondary axis. The wizard introduced organism-level primitives (ChoiceCardGroup, WizardStepper, WizardNavButtons, DocumentUploadZone, DocumentPreview) that followed the codebase’s Feature-Sliced Design pattern. A contextual banner pattern appeared during the same window and got reused on the wallet and product surfaces.

The wizard’s headline PR (a six-figure diff against an empty target) is misleading on its own. The work span lives in the smaller follow-ups, where draft expiry edges, gateway error parsing, redirect guards for the legacy account view, and pending-corrections banners with deep links into the wizard came in one at a time.

Wallet visibility and the four endpoints

Sellers had no way to read their own books on the platform. The work was four Fastify endpoints, a shared payments-core model layer, and the Next.js wallet UI that wires them together. Revenue breakdown. Statement with current balance including affiliate commissions paid by the primary seller. Receivables schedule honouring retention and release rules. Cash-flow aggregation.

The interesting decision was the model layer. The receivables schedule and the cash-flow aggregation needed to agree with what background workers were already computing. Otherwise sellers would see one number in the UI and a different number on the next invoice. Putting the models in payments-core (a shared workspace package) and sharing types between API and worker meant there was exactly one definition of “retained balance” in the codebase. The price was a coordination dance between teams while the model was being agreed. The reward was no follow-up bug about discrepancies, because there was no second definition to disagree.

Fee reconciliation

The platform charges its own fee on top of the payment gateway’s fee. The handling of those two numbers had drifted. The gateway’s platform_fee and platform_fixed_fee were being overwritten by internal default constants whenever the gateway returned a “zero or undefined” value. Low Ticket transactions, legitimately zero-fee by gateway policy, were therefore being booked at the default fee, and nobody could tell from the row.

The fix was small. Stop applying internal fallbacks inside the gateway provider (return undefined when the gateway omits the field), and switch the three transaction-service UPDATE paths from spread-conditional to nullish-coalescing so 0 survives. Five Vitest cases lock the behaviour.

The platform-side counterpart was admin tooling. Exposing the platform’s own fee percentage and fixed fee on the admin client listing, plus a fee column and a non-standard-fee filter so operators could spot divergences without writing a query. A staging mock for the gateway let the whole flow be exercised without touching production data.

Long-tail work

Not every piece of work was a flagship. A lot of the value across the seven months landed in the long tail. Pending-approval banners unified across product, wallet, and KYC with deep links into the right tab. Offer-sync UX with contextual feedback when offers fail to sync with the payment gateway. Phone input standardisation onto an internationalised primitive enforcing E.164. A CPF/CNPJ profile detection fix so legal-entity accounts hit the company data section. Lesson view stabilisation under 4K video, plus a shared workspace package that fixed cache-bust drift across two editors and the member portal.

This is the work that doesn’t make a portfolio screenshot but compounds operationally. The platform is materially better at the seams a year later than it was when I joined. The diff that proves it is spread across hundreds of small PRs, not three big ones.

What I’d want anyone reading this to know

The type-safety pipeline is the work. Everything else in this engagement rests on the chain holding. Cultures that adopt this pipeline win quietly and lose visibly when they break it. Treat every cross-domain change as a verification of the chain, not as code.

Surface the fallback, don’t hide it. Defaults are a feature when they live where you can read them, and a bug when they live behind an adapter that nobody opens. Moving the fee-default decision from the gateway provider to the transaction service made the rule visible, testable, and easy to change. The same pattern repeated for error codes (out of the locale-coupled string-matching layer, into a stable code map). When in doubt, the answer is almost always “surface it, then test it.”

Spec-driven doesn’t mean upfront. The ticket sets the contract. The PR body explains the choice. The commit history carries the intent. None of that is a waterfall. It’s how you make a fast-moving codebase legible six months later, when someone needs to know why fee defaults were moved.

Decisions

Where the work actually got decided.

  1. Nullish-coalescing for fee defaults, not spread-conditional

    Chose
    Switch the three transaction-service `UPDATE` paths to `??`, so a real `0` fee returned by the gateway survives instead of being overwritten by a default constant.
    Over
    Keep the existing spread-conditional pattern, which silently replaced legitimate zero-fee transactions with stale defaults.
    Why
    A Low Ticket case (0% / 0 fixed) was being masked by internal default constants and nobody could tell from the row. The fix was a one-character change to the operator, plus five Vitest cases covering Low Ticket, undefined fallback, custom values, gateway parity, and plan renewal. The deeper decision was where the default lives. Pushing it out of the gateway adapter and into the transaction service made the rule visible, testable, and easy to change. Defaults are a feature when you can read them. They become a bug the moment they hide.

  2. Backend-first KYC wizard with a localStorage draft as fallback

    Chose
    Persist every wizard step to the backend immediately. Fall back to a per-user localStorage draft (7-day TTL) only when the backend cannot yet accept the data.
    Over
    Hold wizard state in memory or localStorage until final submit, then send the full payload.
    Why
    Sellers were losing in-progress work when sessions expired or the gateway rejected a step at the end. Per-step gateway validation surfaces errors when they actually happen, and a per-user draft means a reload never costs anything. The price is more network chatter. The reward is that no submitted payload arrives only to have the gateway reject it.

  3. Stable error codes, not localised string matching

    Chose
    Match against the payment gateway's own error codes in the error-handling layer.
    Over
    Keep matching localised error strings, which is what existed.
    Why
    String matching against translated text is brittle. Any copy change in the gateway breaks our error routing silently. Codes are stable across locales and versions. The migration was unglamorous and removed a class of locale-coupled bugs that were resurfacing every time the gateway updated its messages.
